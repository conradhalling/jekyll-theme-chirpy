I had a summer hiatus of five weeks since my last cello lesson. During this
time, I practiced thirty of the thirty-four days, averaging a little more than
an hour a day on the days I practiced. I keep a detailed practice journal,
and I have a plan for each practice session to make my practicing focused
and efficient.

I normally practice in a room in the finished attic of our house, where I can
close the door. But that room is not air conditioned, and the temperature rises
to 90 °F or even higher on hot summer days. So I have been practicing in the
air conditioned living room, but this is not as convenient and is a little more
public than I like.

I spent the five weeks working slowly on the early pieces in <cite>Suzuki Cello
School, Volume 1</cite>. I really like the approach this book makes, and I made
good progress, but I did not move forward as fast as I thought I might.

I spent $48 on a year-long [makemusic.com](https://makemusic.com) account to
have access to the piano accompaniment for the Suzuki books. I logged in and
tested it using my iPad, and it works fine. The user interface allows me to set
various speeds for the accompaniment.

A significant accomplishment was that I worked on learning to bow without using
the
[Wada Bow Grip]( {% link _posts/2026-08-03-wada-bow-grip.md %} ),
and now I'm comfortable with using a traditional bow hold.

Following general advice to listen to music and watch other cellists, I went to
a
[live concert]( {% link _posts/2026-07-21-morningside-music-bridge.md %} )
of chamber music, and I watched quite a few YouTube videos of cellists. This
has been helpful.

Yesterday, August 14, 2026, I had my ninth cello lesson with
[Ana Ospina](https://www.newschoolofmusic.org/faculty/ospina)
at
[New School of Music](https://www.newschoolofmusic.org).

## Lesson Notes

Ana had me play each piece that I had practiced and gave me pointers and
corrections.

For "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star Variations," Ana had me practice the staccato
eighth notes to make the cello ring out when I stop the bow by using longer bow
strokes and less pressure. She also had me work on playing the sixteenth notes
by holding my bow more loosely and using my wrist and fingers more, making
little circles with the frog to enable playing faster and more clearly.

For "French Folk Song" and "Lightly Row," Ana told me I wasn't saving enough
bow for the longer notes. She wrote dollar signs over the dotted half notes
in "French Folk Song" to remind me to save bow for these long notes.

For "Song of the Wind," there is a part that is tricky for me where I have
to use my fourth finger to play a G on the D string followed immediately by
a D on the A string. From my guitar-playing experience, I pick up my fourth
finger off the D string to move it to the A string, but Ana wants me to
slide the fourth finger smoothly. I couldn't do it during the lesson, so I
will work on that during practice.

I have been practicing "O Come, Little Children," but I can't remember if
Ana had me play it. But for the "Tonalization" exercise on the same page,
Ana had me play louder with long bows, encouraging me to make the cello
resonate so much that I could feel the vibrations of the strings. She also
said that when I change from down stroke to up stroke, I should draw a little
smile with my right elbow to make a smoother transition.

I had jumped ahead a few pieces to "Perpetual Motion in D Major," which
caught my fancy. I have nearly memorized that piece, but I kept getting lost
when I tried to play it for Ana. Ana wants me to practice at 50 bpm and
work my way slowly up to 80 bpm.

Ana wants me to work on my breathing. For example, when I'm starting a 4/4
piece, I need to count one, two, three, and inhale on four.

That was an excellent lesson, and the hour flew by. I have plenty to work on
this week before my next lesson.
