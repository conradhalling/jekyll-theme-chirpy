Cello students are encouraged to watch and listen to other cellists play. With
this justification, I spent nearly $300 to attend opening night of the
[Boston Symphony Orchestra](https://www.bso.org/)
to hear
[Yo-Yo Ma](https://www.yo-yoma.com)
play Edward Elgar's <cite>Cello Concerto in E minor, Opus 85</cite>. (This
was not an economic hardship.)

I was seated on the main floor towards the front and left, in seat J29. This
was fairly close to the stage, a deliberate choice on my part for an excellent
view of Yo-Yo Ma during his performance.

## Speeches and Roses

The sold out performance began with short speeches by Boston’s Mayor
[Michelle Wu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelle_Wu)
(who has played piano as a solist with the BSO) and by
[BSO President and CEO Chad Smith](https://www.bso.org/profiles/chad-smith)
(who read his speech, pretty disappointing for someone with his power
and influence).

Many of the orchestra’s musicians wore a red rose, as did
[Conductor Andris Nelsons](https://www.bso.org/profiles/andris-nelsons),
and there were five red roses on the harp. The red roses signify
support for Nelsons, whose contract was not extended after this season by Chad
Smith and the board of the BSO, eliciting dismay from most of the orchestra's
musicians and many patrons.

Nelsons also gave a short speech, which was somewhat clumsy because English is
not his first language, in which he talked about the red rose as a symbol of
love and respect.

For background on the turmoil at the BSO, read
["The Bitter Fight for Boston’s Symphony Hall"](https://www.bostonmagazine.com/arts-entertainment/2026/07/16/bso-symphony-hall-crisis-hostetter-chad-smith-nelsons/)
by Tom McGrath. I have my own opinions, which I may write about in a future
post.

A side-effect of this dispute is that ticket sales are down, making it easy
to buy a good seat at the last minute, and I plan to take advantage of that
this season.

## Tango

The first piece was
<cite>[Tango](https://www.bso.org/works/maskats-tango)</cite>
(2002) by Latvian composer
[Arturs Maskats](https://www.lmic.lv/en/composers/arturs-maskats-297#work),
featuring
[Julien Labro](https://julienlabro.com)
on bandoneon. According to the program notes, Arturs Maskats and Andris Nelsons
are longtime friends, colleagues, and collaborators. I enjoyed that piece, which
is only twelve minutes long, and I would like to hear it again. Unfortunately
for me, Labro was seated in the middle of the orchestra, and I couldn't see him
from my seat.

Maskats, the composer, came out on stage for a bow. The program notes list many
of Maskats’s other compositions; he is 69 years old and has had a long career.
Why haven’t I heard more of him? This was a good programming choice. I searched
[Idagio](https://www.idagio.com/us)
and found the following recordings:

<cite>Arturs Maskats, Tango</cite> (2002)\
Andris Poga, Latvian National Symphony Orchestra, Artūrs Noviks (Accordion)\
2022, Riga, Great Guild Concert Hall

<cite>Sommernachtskonzert 2022 / Summer Night Concert 2022</cite>\
Andris Nelsons, conductor, Wiener Philharmoniker

<cite>Maskats: Chamber Music</cite> (2003)\
Riga Chamber Players

## Elgar's Cello Concerto

The second piece, the one I bought my ticket for, was Edward Elgar’s
<cite>[Cello Concerto in E minor, Opus 85](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cello_Concerto_(Elgar))</cite>,
in four movements, with Yo-Yo Ma on cello. What I know about this concerto is
that
[Jacqueline du Pré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacqueline_du_Pr%C3%A9)
 made this concerto popular in the late 1960s when she was at her brief
professional height. Sixty years later, Yo-Yo Ma is playing it. I have listened
to recordings once or twice, and I am somewhat indifferent to the music as too
emotional for my taste. However, I enjoyed the Elgar as a live performance.

Yo-Yo was clearly enjoying himself on stage, looking at the audience, other
members of the orchestra, and even waggling his eyebrows at Nathan Cole, the
concertmaster. Yo-Yo, deservedly, is loved by the audience.

The program notes by Hugh Macdonald called this cello concerto “one of the
handful of supreme concertos for the instrument.” After reading the program
notes, I want to explore more of Elgar’s music:

-   Enigma Variations (1899)
-   The Dream of Gerontius
-   Sea Pictures
-   Pomp and Circumstance
-   In the South
-   Introduction and Allegro for strings
-   First Symphony
-   Violin Concerto
-   Second Symphony
-   Falstaff
-   Violin Sonata
-   String Quartet
-   Piano Quintet

After the conclusion of the Elgar concerto and a well-deserved standing ovation,
Yo-Yo Ma came back on stage to join the cello section as the last chair. The
section played a short piece that I learned from reviews was the traditional
Catalan Christmas song
<cite>[El cant dels ocells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_cant_dels_ocells)</cite>
(<cite>The Song of the Birds</cite>), made famous by the cellist
[Pablo Casals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Casals).

## Brahms Fourth Symphony

The
<cite>[Symphony No. 4 in E minor, Op. 98](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._4_(Brahms))</cite>
by
[Johannes Brahms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Brahms) was the only
piece on the second half of the bill. I am not a sophisticated listener of
symphonic music, so I don’t consider my opinion of much value here, but I found
this symphony uninteresting. My feeling when listening was that Brahms chose an
étude, added some variations to it, then said to himself, “Okay, I’m out of
ideas for this section. Let’s choose another étude.” To me, it felt like he
strung these together to make four movements. If he wanted some dynamics, he
added some kettledrums, which are too bombastic for my taste. I felt like the
symphony had three endings, with “dum dum dum dum” on the kettledrums as the
orchestra finished a movement with a loud tonic chord.

Perhaps my opinion is correct: The program notes quote Brahms as saying modestly
and self-deprecatingly, “Once again I’ve just thrown together a bunch of polkas
and waltzes.” But two listeners behind me thought the symphony was excellent and
the performance top rate.

The BSO had played the Brahms Fourth in the spring of 2026. If I had attended
that concert, I would have been disappointed to hear this piece again so soon. I
wonder if Nelsons chose this piece as being familiar and not requiring much
rehearsal time for the opening concert.
