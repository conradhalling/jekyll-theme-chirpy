Last night, November 5, 2026, I attended a concert of the
[Boston Symphony Orchestra](https://www.bso.org/) led by guest conductor
[Philippe Jordan](https://philippejordan.net/)
at Symphony Hall in Boston, Massachusetts. The BSO and the
Tanglewood Festival Chorus performed the oratorio
<cite>[The Creation (Die Schöpfung)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Creation_(Haydn))</cite>
by the Austrian composer
[Joseph Haydn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Haydn).

The soloists were Louise Alder (soprano), Bogdan Volkov (tenor), and David
Steffens (bass-baritone). The enormous Tanglewood Festival Chorus was prepared
by BSO Director of Choruses Jean-Sébastien Vallée. Jan Swafford wrote the
informative and enlightening program notes.

I had never heard <cite>The Creation</cite> before, and when I learned from
the Wikipedia article that the piece is "considered by many to be [Haydn's]
masterpiece," I decided I should hear it.

The music was marvelous, and the soloists, chorus, orchestra, and conductor were
excellent. The libretto, sung in German with English supertitles, covers the
creation story from <cite>Genesis</cite> from the very beginning through the
creation of Adam and Eve and their delight with the new world. (There was only
the slightest hint that things might go wrong later.) Haydn's music contains
a number of surprises and delights, especially when the choir sings, "Let there
be light!"

According to the program, the last performance of this work by the BSO took
place in 1998. I partly understand why this oratorio is infrequently performed.
This is a long work: Parts I and II required one hour twenty minutes, after
which there was an intermission for twenty minutes, and Part III lasted another
thirty minutes. The piece requires three soloists and a full chorus, but the
orchestra itself was not large (only six cellos and four basses). There were two
fortepianos (made by
[R. J. Regier, Freeport, Maine](https://rjregierfortepianos.com/))
on stage, one played by Philippe Jordan during the recitatives. I would like to
hear <cite>The Creation</cite> again, but if I have to wait twenty-eight years
for the next performance, I will no longer be alive (I would have to live to be
a hundred years old).

Stephen Martorella reviewed the performances of <cite>The Creation</cite> in
[The Boston Musical Intelligencer](https://classical-scene.com/2026/11/07/creation-week/).

Next week, as a complement to this concert, Eric Jacobsen conducts the world
premiere of Osvaldo Golijov’s <cite>Creation</cite>, "an evening-length BSO
commission that includes soloists from diverse genres, two choruses, and
original animation and projections."

## Seating in Symphony Hall

I am experimenting with different seating locations during this season at
Symphony Hall. For the first two concerts I attended this fall, I bought
very expensive seats on the main floor close to the stage so I could watch the
soloists. For this concert, I chose seat A25 in the second balcony on the left
side of Symphony Hall. The view was partially blocked by the safety railing.

![Seated View from Seat A25 in the Second Balcony Left Side](symphony_hall_balcony_2_left_a_25.png){:width="800" height="600"}_Seated View from Seat A25 in the Second Balcony Left Side_

With some leaning, I could view the entire stage. All of my neighbors were
leaning at times to see.

![Leaning View from Seat A25 in the Second Balcony Left Side](symphony_hall_balcony_2_left_a_25_lean.png){:width="800" height="600"}_Leaning View from Seat A25 in the Second Balcony Left Side_

Since I had arrived thirty minutes before the concert and most seats were as yet
unoccupied, I tested sitting in the second row, row B, which is up two steps
from row A, but the view was the same.

The sound was excellent; my experience is that the sound is good in any seat in
the hall. I think sitting in the second balcony is perfectly fine when I'm more
interested in hearing than seeing a concert.
