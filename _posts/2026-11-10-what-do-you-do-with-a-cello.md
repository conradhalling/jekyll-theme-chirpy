[Nathan Waks](https://www.octarr.com/artists/nathan-waks) is a well-known
Australian cellist, composer, and producer who owned an
[18th–century master cello](https://www.octarr.com/projects/carcassi-cello)
currently believed to have been made in Florence in 1740–1760 by
[Lorenzo and Tomaso Carcassi](https://tarisio.com/archive/maker/1426).
After playing the cello for forty years, Waks did not want to
sell the cello to a collector who would lock the cello away in a vault; he
wanted the cello to be played.

Waks's solution was to become co-founder and chairman of
[OCTARR](https://www.octarr.com/octarr-origins),
a company that allows patrons to invest in collectable art and musical
instruments. In effect, multiple patrons now own Waks's cello, which is loaned
to a musician for a period of time of at least two years.

The first musician to receive the cello on loan is
[Jung A. Kim](https://www.octarr.com/story/jung-a-kim-receives-nathan-waks-cello),
the winner of inaugural
[Classic Cello International Competition](https://classiccello.com) in London.
Jung A. Kim is shown playing the cello in a
[short video](https://www.octarr.com/story/air-on-a-c-string). Ms. Kim also has
her own [YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@j_a__kim).

The cello was evaluated and certified by
[Florian Leonhard](https://www.florianleonhard.com/),
who was interviewed by OCTARR as part of OCTARR's 18–century Cello Project:

{% include embed/youtube.html id='lb9kjIv0gzU' %}

I learned from this video that the wood in cellos and other string instruments
can be dated using
[dendrochronology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendrochronology).
For example, if the label in the cello states that the cello was made in 1690
but the wood is dated by dendrochronology to 1720, then the label is proven
false.

## What to Do with My Cello?

This story of what to do with your cello when you get old caught my interest
because at age 72 I am the owner of a fine, modern cello that was made by
[Paul Schuback](https://www.schubackviolin.com/)
in 1973. My mother played this cello until the start of the Covid-19 pandemic in
March 2020, when she was 88. After my mother's death at age 91 in 2022, my brothers
and I
attempted unsuccessfully for three years to sell the cello on consignment
through a luthier in the Pacific Northwest and then here through my local
luthier here in the Boston metropolitan area. During this time, I was growing
increasingly unhappy that the cello was not being used.

I decided to learn to play the cello myself, as documented in detail in this
blog. I bought my brothers' shares of the cello at the appraised value, becoming
the sole owner. My plan, while learning to play the cello, is to make contacts
in the cello community here in the Boston metropolitan area to find someone
willing to buy it (possibly at a steep discount) or to donate the cello to a
youth ensemble. My goal is to make sure the cello is being played. This plan is
actually going well, but for now I expect to play the cello myself for a while
since I'm enjoying learning and playing.
