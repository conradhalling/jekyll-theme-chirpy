## Introduction

Yesterday afternoon, November 13, 2026, I went to <cite>A Golden Wire:
Timaeus</cite> at the [Boston Athanaeum](https://bostonathenaeum.org/), a
private library on Beacon Hill. My wife and I are members of the Athenaeum,
which enabled me to reserve my ticket early; the performance sold out almost
immediately.

## A Golden Wire

[A Golden Wire](https://www.agoldenwire.com/about/the-ensemble) is an ensemble
based in New York:

> A Golden Wire specializes in 17th century music of England and the British
> Isles, where the harp and viol were particularly prominent. The ensemble’s
> repertoire also spans further, from the German Renaissance, to the high
> Baroque in France and Italy.

The [ensemble](https://www.agoldenwire.com/about/the-ensemble) includes:

Parker Ramsay, harp\
Arnie Tanimoto, viola da gamba\
Thomas Fields, viola da gamba\
Lucine Musaelian, viola da gamba

## Timaeus

[Timaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timaeus_(dialogue)) refers to a dialog
by Plato that discusses the creation of the earth, heaven, gods, humans, and
animals.

## The Viola da Gamba

I learned recently that the viola da gamba is a member of the viol family. The
viol family is distinct from the violin family, which includes the violin,
viola, and cello. (The terminology and classification of these instruments is
confused and confusing.)

The viola da gamba is also called the viol; viols have multiple strings tuned
mostly in fourths, frets, sloping shoulders at the top of the instrument, and a
flat back. Members of the violin family are tuned in fifths, have flat
shoulders, rounded backs, and no frets.

The double bass has a blend of these characteristics, being tuned in fourths and
having sloping shoulders like a viol but having a rounded back and no frets,
like a member of the violin family.

As I pursue learning the cello, I have become more interest in period
instruments such as the viola da gamba, and consequently I was eager to attend
this concert where I could hear three violas da gamba.
