Last night, Saturday, November 14, 2026, I attended the third and culminating
performance of Osvaldo Golijov's massive new piece, <cite>Creation</cite>,
played by the Boston Symphony Orchestra at Symphony Hall in Boston,
Massachusetts. This piece, with a length of 115 minutes, was commissioned by the
BSO as part of its <cite>Creation Festival</cite>, which included Haydn's
<cite>The Creation</cite> [a week ago]( {% link
_posts/2026-11-06-haydns-creation-at-bso.md %} ) and other smaller concerts and
lectures, including <cite>A Golden Wire: Timaeus</cite>, which [I attended]( {%
link _posts/2026-11-14-timaeus.md %} ) at the Boston Athenaeum on Friday
afternoon.

This oratorio for soloists, choruses, and orchestra featured
[Cécile McLorin Salvant](https://www.cecilemclorinsalvant.com/) (vocalist),
[Davóne Tines](https://alsoanoperasinger.org/) (bass-baritone),
[Cyro Baptista](https://www.cyrobaptista.com/) (narration and percussion),
[Kayhan Kalhor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kayhan_Kalhor)
([kamancheh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamancheh)),
the Tanglewood Festival Chorus, the
BSO x NEC Honors Children's Choir, and
[Eric Jacobsen](https://www.jacobseneric.com/) conducting the BSO for
the first time. The program included the lyrics by Leah Hager Cohen and
superb notes by Robert Kirzinger, the BSO's Director of Program Publications.

The vocalists Cécile McLorin Salvant and Davóne Tines were excellent. Actually,
I think Davóne Tines was amazing with his extremely wide range, precision, and
musical expression. Cyro Baptista was present only for the first half, deploying
a wide range of sometimes odd percussion instruments while also narrating.

The music was accompanied by animations by Alex Sopp, original artwork and
animation, and Camilla Tassi, projection designer; the animations were projected
on a screen above the orchestra; some of the animations were sublime, others
somewhat obvious and ordinary.

The piece is broken into two parts. The first part covers the creation of the
world from multiple perspectives: "Tell Me...," "Come, Sprout, Little
Light-Drinking Child" (Leah Hager Cohen), "Methuselah" (Leah Hager Cohen) —
creation during geologic time —, "Voluspa" (Old Icelandic Saga), "Blue
Garden/Rose Curve" (Leah Hager Cohen), "Alas, pa'volar" (Frida Kahlo), "The Last
Moho Braccatus," "Bahamut" (Leah Hager Cohen), "The Spirit of God Hovering Over
the Waters" (Ecclesiastes), and "The Push" (Ecclesiastes, Leah Hager Cohen).

After the intermission, the second part covers humanity and our prevalence for
violence and cruelty as well as love. This included "First Fruits" (Leah Hager
Cohen) — the story of Cain and Abel —, "Laika" (Leah Hager Cohen) — the
heart-breaking story of Laika, the Russian dog sent into space with no chance of
return —, "La Cueva de las Manos," and finally a glorious and uplifting finale,
"Vincent Starbound, Part I" (Vincent van Gogh, Bible, Dante), "Vincent
Startbound, Part II" (Leah Hager Cohen, Vincent van Gogh, Dante), and
"Everything in Motion" (Leah Hager Cohen).

The "Methuselah" part contains some very beautiful music sung by Davóne
Tines, and other parts are very fine. I personally wouldn't have included the
story of Cain and Abel in a story of creation since there are many stories that
in my opinion are much more uplifting and positive.

At 115 minutes, this piece is self-indulgent and overlong in places. Perhaps
Golijov will do a little pruning if he revises the piece before it is performed
elsewhere. Technically, some of the music was overamplified with occasional poor
balance, and it was so loud that my Apple watch alerted me to dangerously high
noise at one point.

But on the whole, I thought the performance was marvelous and memorable, and
I wish I could hear it again now that I've had time to mentally review my
experience. This was a thrilling experience.
